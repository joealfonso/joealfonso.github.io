Designing the eval: the basics of AI evaluation for people who shape model behavior

What I learned building a field guide on how AI evaluation goes wrong, and why most of those failures are design decisions nobody owned.

Author

Joseph Alfonso

Category

AI Design

Published

Nov 22, 2026

Reading time

9 min

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”

Marilyn Strathern (1997), restating Charles Goodhart

Most of the hard decisions in an AI product are evaluation decisions. Someone decides what “good” means, who checks it, how many times, and against what. If a designer isn’t part of those calls, an engineer, a deadline, or a leaderboard makes them by default, and the experience inherits whatever got picked.

I built the AI Evaluation Field Guide to have one place to point people when this comes up. It’s 26 short patterns for the ways evaluation goes wrong, each tied to the research behind it. This post is the design half of that guide. If your job is shaping how a model behaves, writing the rubric, or deciding what a person sees when the model is wrong, these are the basics I’d want you walking in with.

One note before starting. Fourteen of the 26 pattern names are mine, not established terms. When you take any of this into a review, cite the papers, not my labels.

The rubric is a spec

Every eval is a proxy. A coding benchmark stands in for useful software. A helpfulness score stands in for whether a person got what they came for. The trouble starts when people, or the model itself, push on the proxy. The number moves and the thing behind it doesn’t.

This isn’t a theoretical worry. Gao, Schulman, and Hilton (2023) optimized a model against a learned reward model and tracked a separate “gold” score the whole time. The gold score went up, then turned and came back down while the proxy kept climbing. Pan, Bhatia, and Steinhardt (2022) found that more capable agents were often better at exploiting a badly specified reward, not worse.

For a designer, the takeaway is that a rubric is a behavior spec. Writing “concise” or “acknowledges uncertainty” into a grading rubric shapes what ships just as much as a component spec shapes a screen. It deserves the same rigor: clear definitions, examples of pass and fail, and someone accountable for changing it.

The habit I push for is asking one question before anyone celebrates a jump in a score: what’s the cheapest way to raise this number? If the answer is something users would hate, like longer answers that pad for a length-loving grader, the rubric is wrong. Then check a second measure that nobody was optimizing, and read a sample of the actual outputs. A person reading twenty transcripts catches things a dashboard never will.

Read outputs before you define good

Teams usually write criteria first and look at outputs second. It tends to go the other way. Shankar and colleagues (2024) watched practitioners grade model outputs and found that grading changed what they thought “good” meant. Criteria like tone, or whether an answer admits what it doesn’t know, showed up late, after people had read enough examples to notice them. They called it criteria drift.

Designers know this pattern from research synthesis. You don’t write the headings on the affinity map before you’ve read the sticky notes. The same discipline applies here: open-code a few dozen real outputs before writing a rubric, then version the rubric with a date and a reason for every change. Keep a fixed reference set and re-grade it whenever the rubric moves, so you never compare a score from version two against one from version five as if nothing changed.

I’ll be upfront about the evidence. In the guide I rate this pattern Thin. The study covered nine practitioners on one task, and it didn’t test the fixes I just listed. I kept it because the advice is cheap and the failure it prevents is common.

The people grading are a measurement too

A rubric gets applied by people, and people disagree. Northcutt, Athalye, and Mueller (2021) found label errors in at least 3.3% of examples across ten widely used test sets, and correcting them was enough to change which models ranked higher. Aroyo and Welty (2015) argued that disagreement between raters isn’t noise to average away. It often points at a question with more than one defensible answer.

That’s where a model designer earns their seat. Have two people label the same sample on their own, then measure how often they agree with a chance-corrected statistic like Cohen’s kappa. Keep the disagreements. The cases where two careful raters split are usually the cases where the behavior hasn’t been designed yet.

Test the person and the model together

Most AI products keep a person in the loop, which means the outcome comes from the pair, not the model. This is the pattern I think designers should care about most, because the interface is what decides whether a person catches a wrong answer or waves it through.

The research here is sobering. Vaccaro, Almaatouq, and Malone (2024) pooled 106 studies and found that, on average, human and AI combinations did worse than the better of the two working alone. The pairs lost ground on decision tasks and gained on content creation. Bansal and colleagues (2021) found that adding AI explanations made people more likely to accept the AI’s advice whether the advice was right or not. Explanations raised trust, not accuracy.

There is a design lever that helped. Buçinca, Malaya, and Gajos (2021) tested cognitive forcing functions, small interaction patterns that make a person commit to their own judgment before seeing the AI’s, and found they cut overreliance compared with simple explanations. People liked them less. That tradeoff between comfort and correctness is a design call, and it should be made on purpose.

In practice: compare human alone, AI alone, and the pair. Measure accuracy specifically on the cases where the AI is wrong, since that’s where overreliance shows up. Track time, effort, and confidence alongside correctness. Treat every explanation pattern as an experiment, not a fix.

Trust doesn’t know where the edge is

Dell’Acqua and colleagues ran field experiments with 758 consultants at Boston Consulting Group. On tasks inside what they called the jagged frontier, people using GPT-4 finished 12.2% more tasks, worked 25.1% faster, and produced work rated roughly 40% higher. On one task built so the model would get it wrong, 84.5% of the people without AI got it right, against roughly 60% to 70% of the people with it.

AI capability is uneven, and its edges don’t line up with what people find hard. Trust earned on the tasks it handles well carries over to the ones it doesn’t. The design work is mapping that edge task by task, including tasks that seem too easy to test, showing uncertainty near the edges, and redoing the map every time the model changes. The study covered one firm, one model, and one trap task, so treat it as a reason to test your own tasks, not as a number to quote.

One good answer isn’t a behavior

A lot of evals run each case once, or report the best of several tries. That tells you what a model can do. It doesn’t tell you what a person will get on Tuesday when they ask the same thing they asked on Monday.

The difference has a name. Pass@k counts a case as solved if any one of k tries succeeds. Pass^k counts it only if all k succeed. Yao and colleagues (2025) built τ-bench to simulate agents helping simulated customers. GPT-4o solved about 61% of retail tasks on a single try. When the question became whether it solved the same task on all eight of eight tries, the rate fell to roughly a quarter.

Inconsistency is an experience problem before it’s a metrics problem. A person who gets a great answer once and a confused one the next time stops trusting both. When I review an eval plan I ask for each case to run at least three times, with both the average success rate and the all-runs-pass rate reported. I also ask for paraphrased versions of each task, because a score from one prompt describes that prompt and not much else. Where to set the bar depends on the cost of a failure. A brainstorming tool can live with variance. A tool that answers benefits questions can’t.

The average is hiding somebody

A 95% overall score can be 99% for most people and 70% for a small group. Those groups are rarely random. In Gender Shades, Buolamwini and Gebru (2018) tested three commercial gender classifiers with overall accuracy between 87.9% and 93.7%. Error rates reached 34.7% for darker-skinned women and stayed at or under 0.8% for lighter-skinned men. The headline number described nobody in particular.

Oakden-Rayner and colleagues (2020) found the same shape in medical imaging. A model for detecting collapsed lungs scored far better on X-rays that showed a chest drain, a sign the patient was already being treated, than on X-rays without one. The cases that mattered most were the ones it handled worst.

To me this is accessibility work in a different form. We don’t sign off on an interface because the average user finished the task. We test with screen readers, low vision, and slow connections because that’s where things break. Evals need the same instinct. Define the groups that matter before you look at the overall number, report the worst group next to the average, and read failures by hand to find the groups you didn’t think to define.

The lab isn’t the field

Beede and colleagues (2020) followed a diabetic eye screening system into 11 clinics in Thailand. Lighting, workflow, and time pressure all affected how it performed, and its image quality rules rejected photos the clinics could realistically take. The model had been validated. The system hadn’t.

Evaluating only the model is like usability testing only the backend. Designers already own the methods that close this gap: contextual inquiry, diary studies, small pilots before a wide rollout, and defining success with the people affected, including the ones who never touch the interface.

Lessons learned

Building the guide changed how I approach AI design reviews. These are the parts I use most.

  1. Treat the rubric as a design artifact. It specifies behavior the same way a component spec does. Give it an owner, examples of pass and fail, and a version history with reasons.
  2. Read outputs before you write criteria. What “good” means shows up in the transcripts. Keep a fixed reference set so a rubric change never passes for a model improvement.
  3. Score the pair, not the model. Compare human alone, AI alone, and both together, and look hardest at the cases where the model is wrong. That’s where the interface either helps or hurts.
  4. Put the worst number next to the headline. Report the all-runs-pass rate next to the average and the worst group next to the overall score. If a readout only has one number, ask what it left out.
  5. Label your confidence. Every pattern in the guide carries an evidence label: Well sourced, Developing, or Thin. Two of the patterns in this post, criteria drift and the pass^k idea, are rated Thin, and I say so on the page. An eval readout should do the same, so the person making the decision knows how hard to lean on it.
